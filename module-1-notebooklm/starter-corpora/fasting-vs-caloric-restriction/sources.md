# Starter corpus: Intermittent fasting, time-restricted eating, and plain caloric restriction

**11 sources.** All verified against PubMed on 2026-08-24.

> **Why this corpus is the best one in the set for learning the skill.**
>
> The trials here **genuinely contradict each other**, and not because anyone did bad work. Several
> well-conducted randomised trials found that time-restricted eating adds nothing once calories are
> matched. Others found real benefit. Three meta-analyses published within a year of each other
> reach different emphases from overlapping evidence.
>
> That is the situation a grounded notebook is actually for, and it is the situation where an
> ungrounded one is most dangerous, because it will hand you a confident one-sentence answer to a
> question the field has not settled.
>
> It is also a topic everyone in the room has an opinion about, which makes the disagreement easy
> to feel rather than merely observe.

## The shape of the disagreement

Load these and the argument organises itself:

**"It is just the calories"** ← Trepanowski, Lowe, Liu, Templeman
**"There is something beyond the calories"** ← Stekovic, Teong, Pavlou
**"Here is what the whole literature says"** ← Semnani-Azad, Khalafi, Siles-Guerrero

Loading both sides is the point. A notebook holding only one side will sound just as confident as
one holding both.

## The two questions

### Question A, the one it should answer precisely

> **In the Liu et al. NEJM trial, what were the calorie targets in each arm, what was the eating
> window in the time-restricted arm, and what was the difference in weight loss between arms at 12
> months?**

Specific numbers from one paper. **Click the citation** and confirm each one. The interesting part
is that the calories were matched by design, which is exactly why the null result means something
and why quoting "TRE does not work" without that detail is a misreading.

### Question B, the one it should refuse

> **Is intermittent fasting safe for a patient on insulin, and how should the regimen be adjusted?**

Nothing here is a clinical management guideline. A grounded notebook should decline rather than
improvise dosing advice, and this is a pointed refusal because it is the question a patient will
actually ask you.

**Spare question B:** what does the American Diabetes Association currently recommend about
meal timing? No guideline is in this corpus.

## The exercise this corpus exists for

> **Does time-restricted eating produce more weight loss than ordinary caloric restriction?**

Ask it. Then read what comes back very carefully, because there are three quite different answers a
tool could give:

1. **It picks a side** and gives you one confident answer. Check which sources it cited. It has
   almost certainly under-weighted the ones that disagree, and you would never know without
   looking.
2. **It lays out both positions with citations** and tells you the trials conflict. This is the
   correct answer, and it is what grounding buys you.
3. **It gives you the meta-analysis conclusion** as though it settled things. Better, but still
   worth clicking through: a network meta-analysis compares strategies that were never actually
   trialled against each other, which is a real caveat that a summary tends to drop.

**Then ask the follow-up that separates a useful tool from an impressive one:**

> "Which of my sources disagree with each other, and on what specifically?"

That question is the whole module in one line. Whatever it answers, click through and decide for
yourself. The judgement is the skill. The tool is just what makes the judgement cheap enough to
exercise.
