Lightweight automation: biosketches and CVs

Small, safe automations that save time without programming. Your publication list fetched rather than typed, your ORCID checked the way NIH will read it, and a draft you can verify line by line, because you are the one who signs it.

Session 1 Fri 9 Oct Session 2 Thu 15 Oct 12:00 to 13:00 Education 2 North

Before you come · takes five minutes

1. Find your ORCID iD. Sign in at orcid.org. It is the sixteen-digit number under your name, like 0000-0001-2345-6789. No ORCID yet? Creating one takes two minutes, and NIH requires it anyway.

2. Check you can open Microsoft Copilot. Go to copilot.cloud.microsoft and sign in with your CU account. Everyone at CU has it, free.

3. Optional: bring a specific aims page from a current or planned grant, as text you can copy and paste.

4. No biosketch of your own? If you coordinate or support research, bring the ORCID iD of a PI you work with. ORCID iDs are public, and you will leave with a list of what their record is missing.

01

What you will do

  1. Watch a chat tool invent a citation, then look it up

    Before anything useful, we ask an ungrounded chat tool to write a biosketch Personal Statement with PMIDs, and then look the PMIDs up in PubMed. It takes ten seconds and it is the reason for everything else in the hour.

  2. Fetch your record instead of typing it

    Your ORCID iD goes in and your real publications come back, with DOIs and PMIDs. This is the part of a CV that changes every month, and nobody should be retyping it.

  3. Find out what NIH will find

    SciENcv pre-populates your biosketch from ORCID and your other records. The page checks what it will see: publications without identifiers, positions with no end date, and the sections nothing can fill for you.

  4. Rank your publications against a project

    Paste your aims and your publications are scored by the words they share, with the matching words shown. There is no AI in this step, so you can see exactly why each paper ranked where it did. The top ten are your shortlist for the Products list.

  5. Draft, then check every citation

    The page writes a prompt that carries your real publications. Paste it into Microsoft Copilot, which everyone at CU has, or CU ChatGPT Edu. Every PMID in the draft has to be one you supplied. Anything else is a fabrication you were about to certify.

03

What changed at NIH

  • Since 25 January 2026, NIH has required the Common Forms for the biographical sketch, made in SciENcv. Since 8 May 2026, a submission without them is stopped by the system rather than flagged.
  • SciENcv pre-populates the Common Form (identifying information, professional preparation, appointments, and up to ten Products) from eRA Commons, ORCID and My Bibliography. NIH asks you to review what it brings in.
  • You still write the Supplement: a Personal Statement of up to 3,500 characters, up to 15 honors, and up to five Contributions to Science of up to 2,000 characters each.
  • Those ten Products are the only works you may cite, using short in-text references such as a PMID in parentheses. No full citations, no reference list, no links.
  • Your ORCID iD must be linked to your eRA Commons account, or the documents cannot be submitted.
  • And you certify it yourself: that it is current, accurate and complete, along with statements on foreign talent recruitment programs and research security training.

The authority is NIH, not this page: the Biographical Sketch Common Form, NOT-OD-26-018 and NOT-OD-26-079. Checked on 7 October 2026.

04

Beyond the biosketch

The biosketch is the worked example. The same shape, a fixed input, fixed steps and an output you check, fits a great deal of research administration.

The cookbook has twenty-three of them: bios, progress-report tables, email digests, meeting notes, deadline trackers, forms that log themselves, and a collection box for biosketches. Most run in Microsoft Copilot, which everyone at CU has, and a few in Power Automate, which keeps working while you are not looking.

05

Between the two sessions

Fill in five more sections of your record. Then either render a CU-format CV you would actually send, or draft one Contribution to Science and check every PMID in it. Bring whichever you did, and bring one thing the tool got wrong.

Download your record before you leave on Friday. Your work is saved in the browser you used, but if you come back on a different laptop, Open a saved record brings it back from the file.

The mistakes are the most useful thing you can bring. Session two spends ten minutes on what the tools got wrong, and those examples teach the room more than the successes do.

06

The one rule

You sign it

A draft from a chat tool is a draft. What you certify to NIH is yours.

Check every citation, every date and every claim before it goes anywhere near SciENcv. The rule from Module 1 still holds: click the citation.

A biosketch holds no patient data. Microsoft Copilot Chat and CU ChatGPT Edu, signed in with your CU account, are both approved for University data up to Highly Confidential. Still no PHI, anywhere. The authority is CU's AI tool overview and comparison, not this page.

The record builder has no account, no upload and no key. The only things that leave your browser are your ORCID iD and publication titles, sent to ORCID, Crossref and PubMed to look up identifiers.
